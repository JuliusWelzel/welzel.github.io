Bayesian integration in foot placement control

Project · Gait · Computational motor control

Where to put the next foot

Every step is a decision made under uncertainty. We ask whether the nervous system makes it like a Bayesian observer.

Collaborators Moira van Leeuwen, Sjoerd Bruijn and Jaap van Dieën

Where Department of Human Movement Sciences, Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam

Topic Foot placement control and Bayesian observer models

Status Ongoing

The question

We keep our balance while walking mainly through foot placement. Where the foot lands is adjusted step by step to the state of the body’s centre of mass (CoM). In steady-state walking, a simple linear model of CoM position and velocity during swing predicts a large part of where the next foot will land. The variance it explains is taken as the degree of foot placement control, and its residuals as foot placement error (Wang & Srinivasan, 2014; Bruijn & van Dieën, 2018).

But the nervous system never knows the state of the centre of mass directly. It has to estimate it from noisy visual, vestibular and proprioceptive signals and from its own predictions. Bayesian observer models say how this should be done: weigh each source of information by its reliability, and combine the evidence with prior expectations (Ernst & Banks, 2002; Körding & Wolpert, 2004). Together with Moira van Leeuwen and the Amsterdam group, I ask whether foot placement follows these principles.

If the senses become less reliable, a Bayesian walker should rely less on the momentary state of the body, and more on what it expects.

Approach

A Bayesian account makes testable predictions. If the sensory estimate of body state becomes less reliable, foot placement should depend less on the measured CoM state and shift towards prior expectations, as in the figure above. The project builds on three established lines of work:

  • the CoM-based foot placement model developed in Amsterdam (open-source FootPlacement code by Moira van Leeuwen and Sjoerd Bruijn; van Leeuwen et al., 2020)
  • earlier work that degrades specific sensory cues during walking, for example with muscle vibration (Arvin et al., 2018) or vestibular stimulation (Magnani et al., 2021)
  • observer models of state estimation and sensory integration in balance control (Kuo, 2005; Maeda et al., 2017)

Moira and I also work together in StepuP, where the Amsterdam work package studies whether treadmill training in Parkinson’s disease improves foot placement coordination and the estimation of the body’s state. We organised the first joint StepuP meeting at VU Amsterdam in February 2024.
