StepuP: Steps against the burden of Parkinson’s disease

Project · Parkinson’s disease · Mobile EEG

StepuP

A multicentre randomised controlled trial on the biomechanical and neurophysiological mechanisms of speed-dependent treadmill training in Parkinson’s disease.

Funding EU Joint Programme – Neurodegenerative Disease Research (JPND), JPND2022-128

Period 2023 – 2026

Consortium Six partners in the Netherlands, Germany, Italy, Israel, Australia and Switzerland, coordinated by Jaap van Dieën (VU Amsterdam)

My role Kiel site lead with Walter Maetzler; multimodal recording and data standards for the consortium; shared first author of the protocol

Aims

Speed-dependent treadmill training (SDTT) improves gait in Parkinson’s disease, but the underlying mechanisms are unclear. StepuP tests two hypotheses: that training improves stability-related foot placement, i.e. the step-to-step adjustment of foot placement to the state of the body’s centre of mass, and that it improves cortical sensorimotor integration, measured as gait-related EEG beta power and EEG–EMG (corticomuscular) coherence. Secondary aims are to compare standard SDTT with an enriched protocol (SDTT+, with virtual reality or mechanical perturbations) and to assess transfer to walking in daily life.

Design

StepuP consists of three harmonised, parallel randomised controlled trials with a shared core protocol at clinical sites in Kiel, Bologna, Tel Aviv and Sydney. Each trial includes 42 people with Parkinson’s disease (126 in total). A reference group of 21 healthy older adults is measured in Amsterdam. Participants are randomised to standard speed-dependent treadmill training (SDTT) or to SDTT+, and complete twelve 30-minute training sessions. Depending on the site, SDTT+ adds virtual-reality obstacles, mechanical belt perturbations, or both.

Assessments take place at baseline, after training and at a 12-week follow-up and comprise:

  • Synchronised 64-channel EEG, EMG and 3D motion capture during treadmill walking at comfortable and fixed speeds, plus resting-state EEG
  • Clinical testing, e.g. MDS-UPDRS III, Mini-BESTest, MoCA, Trail Making Test, Timed Up and Go, falls self-efficacy and quality of life
  • One week of daily-life monitoring with a lower-back inertial sensor (digital mobility outcomes)

The primary clinical endpoint is comfortable overground walking speed. Mechanistic outcomes include stride length and stride time, the explained variance and error of a foot-placement model, EEG beta power and corticomuscular coherence. They are analysed with linear mixed-effects models.

Data acquisition and standardisation

The sites use different EEG, EMG and motion-capture systems. All data streams are synchronised with the Lab Streaming Layer and converted into a common multi-site dataset in the Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS), with eeg, emg and motion data per participant and session, following Motion-BIDS for the motion data.

My role

Together with Walter Maetzler, I lead the Kiel site (University Hospital Schleswig-Holstein and Kiel University). Across the consortium, I am responsible for the multimodal recording and data-standardisation pipeline:

  • LSL synchronisation setup for the EEG, EMG and motion-capture systems
  • EEG data-quality checks
  • BIDS converter for the recordings and REDCap clinical data of all sites
  • EEG and BIDS training sessions at consortium meetings

I am shared first author of the study protocol with Moira van Leeuwen.
